## The Question Everyone Is Asking

If you work in tech, you've probably had this thought at 2 AM: "Will GenAI take my job?"

I've asked myself the same question. I use LLMs daily for coding, writing, and debugging, and I've watched them go from clumsy autocomplete to tools that can build working features in minutes. It's natural to feel both excited and a little nervous.

After spending a lot of time building with these tools, my honest answer is: it's complicated. Some tasks will disappear, some jobs will change shape, and new ones will appear. Panic isn't useful, but neither is pretending nothing is happening.

## Jobs vs Tasks

The most useful shift in my thinking was to stop asking about jobs and start asking about tasks.

A job is a bundle of many tasks. A developer doesn't just "write code." They clarify vague requirements, make design trade-offs, debug strange production issues, review code, communicate with teammates, and decide what *not* to build. GenAI is already good at some of these tasks and still weak at others.

So the real question is: which parts of my work can be automated, and what's left when they are? Usually, what's left is judgment, context, and accountability, and those become more valuable, not less.

## What GenAI Is Already Good At

From my own experience, LLMs are strong at:

- **Boilerplate and repetitive code** — CRUD endpoints, config files, tests, data transformations.
- **First drafts** — emails, documentation, summaries, outlines.
- **Explaining things** — unfamiliar codebases, error messages, new concepts.
- **Translating between formats** — SQL to ORM, Python to TypeScript, notes to structured text.
- **Brainstorming** — listing options and edge cases you might have missed.

If most of your day is spent on these kinds of tasks, your workflow is already changing.

## What GenAI Still Struggles With

Even with impressive progress, I keep running into the same gaps:

- **Missing context.** A model doesn't know your company's history, your users, or why a strange decision was made three years ago.
- **Confident mistakes.** LLMs can produce wrong answers that look completely right. Someone has to verify.
- **Ambiguous goals.** Deciding *what* to build is often harder than building it.
- **Long, messy, real-world systems.** Debugging across services, legacy code, and unclear requirements still needs a human who understands the whole picture.
- **Accountability.** When something breaks in production or a decision affects real people, a human is responsible. A model isn't.

These limits may shrink over time, but they explain why "AI replaces everyone" is too simple a story.

## Which Roles Are Most Exposed?

I don't think it's useful to make precise predictions, because nobody really knows the timeline. But some patterns seem reasonable:

- Work that is **repetitive, well-defined, and easy to check** is the most exposed.
- Work that depends on **trust, relationships, physical presence, or complex judgment** is more resilient.
- **Entry-level work** deserves special attention, since many junior tasks are exactly the kind AI handles well. This may change how people learn and enter the field.

Every industry will feel this differently, so it's worth looking at your own role honestly rather than relying on headlines.

## History Offers Clues, Not Guarantees

Earlier technologies like spreadsheets, calculators, and compilers also changed work. They didn't eliminate whole professions overnight. They automated certain tasks, raised expectations for output, and created new kinds of work.

But history is not a promise. This wave is different in that it touches knowledge work broadly, and it's moving fast. The fair takeaway is that change is likely to be real and uneven, with some people affected much more than others. I'd rather prepare than assume everything will be fine.

## What I'm Doing About It

Here's how I'm approaching it as a developer:

1. **Using the tools seriously.** The people who learn to work well with GenAI will likely outpace those who ignore it or those who trust it blindly.
2. **Going deeper on fundamentals.** Understanding how systems, data, and algorithms really work helps me catch the model's mistakes.
3. **Building judgment.** Choosing the right problem, the right trade-off, and the right level of complexity is hard to automate.
4. **Improving communication.** Explaining ideas clearly to people and to models is becoming a core skill.
5. **Building things end to end.** Projects teach me where AI helps and where it falls short, far better than reading about it.
6. **Staying adaptable.** The tools I use today will probably look different in a year, so I'm focusing on learning how to learn.

## Practical Advice If You're Worried

- Pick one repetitive part of your work and try automating it with an LLM this week.
- Learn to review AI output critically. Verification is a real skill.
- Build a small public portfolio of projects that shows your thinking, not just your code.
- Talk to your team about how to adopt these tools safely and effectively.
- Don't measure your worth by tasks a machine can do. Measure it by the problems you can solve.

## Final Thoughts

Will GenAI take my job? Parts of it, probably yes. All of it? I don't think so, but I also can't promise that, and anyone who does is guessing.

What I do believe is that the safest position isn't denial or fear. It's curiosity, skill-building, and a willingness to adapt. The goal isn't to compete with AI at what it does best, but to become someone who can use it to do more meaningful work.
